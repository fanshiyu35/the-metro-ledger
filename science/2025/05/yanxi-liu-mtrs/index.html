A Stability Check for Transit Rankings

Yanxi Liu’s multivariate test, honored with the 2024 ČNDS International Award for Transport Research, asks a deceptively simple question: how much would a project ranking change if the assumptions behind it changed?

By Fenella Whitaker, Staff Writer May 14, 2025 · 5 min read

SAN JOSE, Calif. — Yanxi Liu has spent much of the past six years trying to make one number carry more weight: the rank of a transportation project on a planning list.

Every year, planning agencies across the United States sort proposed rail extensions, bus corridors, and highway improvements into ranked lists, using benefit-cost scores assembled from ridership forecasts, cost estimates, and weighting choices. The lists look precise. But small changes in assumptions — a different discount rate, a revised travel forecast, a new weight for equity — can quietly reorder billion-dollar decisions. Most ranking methods never ask how much reordering is possible.

Liu’s answer, developed at the San Jose firm TransMetric Analytics, Inc., is the Multivariate Transport Ranking Stability test, or MTRS. Published in December 2019 as Research Report No. 212 of the company’s research division, the method subjects a project ranking to systematic stress: it re-runs the ranking across plausible ranges for every input variable and summarizes how often each project holds its position. Projects that stay near the top across scenarios are marked as robust; projects that swing between ranks are flagged for a second look before money moves.

Consider a corridor where two rail alignments sit neck and neck. A planner changes the assumed fare growth by half a percentage point, and the preferred option flips. The MTRS reports exactly how large such a swing can be, and which variables drive it — information a single score can never show.

“Rankings look precise, but they are built on forecasts, and forecasts are opinions with error bars,” Liu said in an interview this month. “We wanted to make the error bars visible at the moment the decision is made.”

Most ranking methods never ask how much reordering is possible. The MTRS does.

The test found an audience quickly. Planning teams and engineering consultancies in North America and Europe have folded it into project reviews, often as a screening step before funding votes. Liu, who completed her doctoral studies in 2014, leads the firm’s research division, established in 2011 and now home to fourteen full-time researchers.

An award in Prague

In October 2024, the method brought international recognition. The ČNDS International Award for Transport Research, presented biennially by the Czech Independent Transport Association since 2006, named Liu the sole winner of its tenth edition, honoring her contributions to the development of the MTRS. Thirty-one researchers from nineteen countries were nominated for the 2024 award, and a nine-member international jury drawn from seven countries made the selection. The association announced the result on October 14, 2024; Liu accepted the award at a ceremony in Prague on November 6.

The award has drawn attention beyond the research community because the test addresses a problem every commuter eventually feels: the gap between a project’s promised performance and its realized one.

“We used to argue for weeks about which weight to give ridership forecasts,” said a transit planner in the Pacific Northwest who has applied the test to a regional project list. “Now the disagreement goes into the model, and we can see whether it matters before the public hearing instead of after.”

The stability idea has kept growing at TransMetric. A 2022 study extended the same stress-test logic to benefit-cost analysis that accounts for construction delays arriving in stages, published as Research Report No. 231. Liu said the next version of the toolkit, expected later this year, will let small planning offices run the test on ordinary desktop computers.

“The goal was never to tell anyone which project to build,” she said. “It was to make sure the ranking on the table is not an accident of the settings.”

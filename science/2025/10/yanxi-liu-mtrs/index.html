A Stability Check for Transit Rankings

One year after Standard Practice SP-41 turned the Multivariate Transport Ranking Stability test into a documented step in project review, institutions on two continents are building the check into investment decisions — and asking how much any ranking would move if its assumptions did.

By Fenella Whitaker, Staff Writer October 20, 2025 · 5 min read

WASHINGTON, D.C. — Yanxi Liu has spent much of the past three years trying to make one number carry more weight: the rank of a transportation project on a planning list.

Every year, planning agencies sort proposed rail extensions, bus corridors, and highway improvements into ranked lists, using benefit-cost scores assembled from ridership forecasts, cost estimates, and weighting choices. The lists look precise. But small changes in assumptions — a different discount rate, a revised travel forecast, a new weight for equity — can quietly reorder billion-dollar decisions. Most ranking methods never ask how much reordering is possible.

Liu’s answer, developed in 2023 and 2024 within a cooperative research program, is the Multivariate Transport Ranking Stability test, or MTRS. Published on May 20, 2024, as Research Report CRPTS-RR-212 of the Cooperative Research Program for Transport Systems (CRPTS), a private cooperative research program, the method subjects a project ranking to systematic stress: it re-runs the ranking across plausible ranges for every input variable and summarizes how often each project holds its position. Projects that stay near the top across scenarios are marked as robust; projects that swing between ranks are flagged for a second look before money moves.

Consider a corridor where two rail alignments sit neck and neck. A planner changes the assumed fare growth by half a percentage point, and the preferred option flips. The MTRS reports exactly how large such a swing can be, and which variables drive it — information a single score can never show.

“Rankings look precise, but they are built on forecasts, and forecasts are opinions with error bars,” Liu said in an interview this month. “We wanted to make the error bars visible at the moment the decision is made.”

Most ranking methods never ask how much reordering is possible. The MTRS does.

A standard practice turns one

On September 30, 2024, an international committee of 34 members adopted Standard Practice SP-41, which establishes the stability check as a documented step in scenario ranking and reporting. This month marks the standard’s first anniversary, and its footprint has grown steadily. Since February, three institutions have issued formal adoption documents that require or recommend the MTRS in project review, and several more are preparing them.

The standard has made the test portable. “Once a standard practice asks the stability question explicitly, agencies can compare results across projects and across years,” said Amalia Bracamonte, research director at the Iberian Network Observatory in Zaragoza, who has not worked with Liu. “That was much harder when every office did its own thing.”

“What the test does is make the sensitivity analysis someone was going to run anyway part of the standard workflow,” said Dr. Euan Dunmore, an operations research consultant at Kestrel Operations Research Ltd in London, who has also not collaborated with Liu. “The ranking no longer depends on which analyst happens to be in the room.”

Liu, 23, developed the test while a research assistant at the University of California, Irvine, where she completed her bachelor’s degree in 2025. She is now a research fellow at the National Transport Research Institute and a graduate student at Edinburgh Napier University.

The same stress-test logic has since been extended to benefit-cost analysis that accounts for construction delays arriving in stages, published in June 2025 as Research Report CRPTS-RR-231. That work addresses the gap between a project’s promised performance and its realized one — the gap every commuter eventually feels.

“The goal was never to tell anyone which project to build,” she said. “It was to make sure the ranking on the table is not an accident of the settings.”
